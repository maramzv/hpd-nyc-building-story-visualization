The pattern, in five buckets

Every building's open violations get classified into one of five patterns, based on how often a real defect (not a paperwork filing) recurs and for how long:

No real defects19.4% · 32,256 buildings
Every violation on this building's record is administrative: a late registration filing, a missed bedbug report. No physical defect has ever been cited here.
Isolated46.1% · 76,785 buildings
This building has real physical defects on record, but only a handful (below the citywide 75th percentile of 9), and none of them has ever been cited a second time.
Widespread17.7% · 29,479 buildings
This building has many real physical defects on record (9+, more than most buildings with no repeating pattern), but none of them has ever been cited a second time.
Persistent14.1% · 23,453 buildings
The same specific defect has been cited 3 or more times over at least 2 years: it kept coming back after being flagged.
Chronic2.7% · 4,508 buildings
The same specific defect has been cited 10 or more times over at least 5 years: a problem that's gone unresolved for the better part of a decade.

All figures on this page are read live off data/map_dataset.json, the same file the map itself loads, so they move with every re-pull instead of being frozen at whatever they were on launch day.

Recurrence is measured building-wide, not per apartment: a defect (same order code, coherent description) recurring across different units still counts as the same recurring problem. A pest infestation cited in nine different apartments over several years, for example, is one building-wide recurring problem, not nine unrelated ones (see "How this taxonomy was developed" below for how this was validated).

What this reveals, once you zoom in

The headline finding: recurring defects are not spread evenly across the city. Persistent + Chronic buildings run about 2.7× higher in the Bronx and Manhattan (~26%) than in Queens and Staten Island (~10%), and at the neighborhood level, that gap widens further still: nearly 3 in 5 buildings with an open violation in Fordham Heights, Bronx show a recurring defect.

By borough, Persistent + Chronic buildings run roughly 2.7× higher in the Bronx and Manhattan (both ~26%) than in Queens and Staten Island (both ~10%):

Six dimensions, one sentence

Each building's story is composed from six measured dimensions rather than a single fixed label, because the dimensions vary independently: a building can be chronic and recently accelerating and well-handled by the owner, all at the same time, and each of those facts needs to be stated on its own rather than compressed into one word (see "How this taxonomy was developed" below for the validation behind that decision).

Each dimension is explained below on its own terms: what it measures, how it's calculated, where it can mislead if read alone, and a plain example. Four of these six dimensions are a single number sorted into a named tier; the other two (Engagement, Pattern) are a small set of conditions checked together, which is why they don't get a threshold bar.

Pattern × Severity: why crossing two axes isn't multiplication

Crossing two dimensions doesn't mean multiplying them. It means asking one question: of the buildings that landed in Pattern bucket A, how many also carry Severity level B? That question only matters if the two axes can move independently, and the real numbers say they do. Computed directly from the current 166,481-building dataset, using each building's overall Class C share (a simplified rate-only version of Severity; the exact live algorithm also floors any building with a currently-open, uncertified Class C straight to "Elevated," but which violations are uncertified isn't part of this summary file, only the full per-building evidence record):

In plain terms: a building having a chronic, long-running problem doesn't mean it's currently an extreme hazard. Most Chronic buildings actually sit at Low or Elevated severity, not Extreme. And a building can carry an Extreme-severity hazard today with no history of anything repeating at all. Recurrence and danger are answers to two different questions, and the table below is what makes that visible rather than assumed.

10,417 buildings currently carry an Extreme-severity record while sitting in the "Isolated" bucket, more than twice the entire Chronic bucket (4,508). A one-off Class C hazard and a decade-long recurring problem are both real, and both rare relative to the whole dataset, but they are not the same finding, which is exactly why Severity is never folded into Pattern's color.

Meanwhile only 32 of 4,508 Chronic buildings (0.7%) reach Extreme severity. Most Chronic recurrence (65%) is Low or Elevated severity, not the most hazardous class. Recurring for years and being dangerous right now are two different questions.

Show me the records

Every building's detail view includes a "Why we're saying this" link that opens the actual underlying violation records (date, class, status, description) that the story above was generated from. No story on this site is asserted without the evidence being one click away. Each record in that timeline is color-coded by what actually happened to it. The underlying date fields each state draws on, and how long each step of the process typically takes, are broken down in Data:

FixedThe landlord certified the repair was done and the city accepted it. Resolved, at least on record.
City follow-upSomething is still moving: a reinspection scheduled, a warning letter or civil penalty sent, an extension granted. Active, not resolved.
Newly issuedThe fix deadline hasn't passed yet, however short (a Class C can carry a 5-day deadline) or long. Too early to say anything.
Past-dueThe fix deadline has passed with no accepted repair. After ~2 years with nothing further, it becomes Frozen.
FrozenNothing recorded in ~2 years. Could be the landlord ignoring it, could be the city never following up. The records can't say which.
Cert. rejectedThe landlord filed a certification claiming it was fixed, and the city rejected it as false or invalid.
Paperwork not filedNot a repair: a required filing (registration, annual bedbug report, a posted notice) that hasn't been made.

How this taxonomy was developed

The Pattern taxonomy was built and validated in stages, moving from a smaller sample to the full citywide population as each stage's results were tested against real buildings.

Stage 1: initial taxonomy. A three-bucket model (Isolated / Persistent / Chronic) was built on a 2,983-building sample, with recurrence matched within the same apartment. This established the core method: group violations by building, detect repeat citations of the same defect code, and classify by how many times and over how long a span.

Stage 2: full-population validation. Extending the method to all 166,481 buildings surfaced two refinements the sample hadn't shown a need for. First, apartment-scoped matching cannot see a defect recurring across different units in the same building. A pest infestation cited in nine separate apartments over several years is a single building-wide recurring problem, not nine isolated ones. Recurrence matching was extended to the building level accordingly. Second, "Isolated" was carrying two distinct realities under one label: buildings with no physical defect on file at all, and buildings with many different one-off defects but no repeated one. These were split into "No real defects" and "Widespread," producing the current five-bucket model and its citywide distribution above.

Stage 3: the six-dimension model. Testing candidate single-label story types (e.g. "chronic," "acute," "recurring") against a stratified 80-building sample showed the underlying measurements move independently of each other. Scale, recency, severity, and owner engagement do not correlate strongly enough to compress into one label without losing information (2455 Bedford Avenue below is Chronic and Severe scale but also Gone quiet on Recency; a different building can share that same Chronic/Severe combination while actively escalating). The dimensional model replaced the fixed-label approach for this reason: each axis is reported on its own, and the narrative sentence states whichever combination the evidence actually supports.

Order codes that recur by regulatory design rather than by physical defect (an annual bedbug-report filing, a registration renewal) were identified during this validation and excluded from recurrence detection (7 codes currently, audited from the top 60 of 396 by citywide volume; see Known limitations below).

Known limitations

These are open, not hidden. As of this writing:

  • "Open" does not always mean "actively live." Certified-fixed violations are excluded from backlog age, and a violation past deadline with zero recorded activity for 2+ years is labeled "Frozen" in the evidence timeline rather than treated the same as an actively-contested one. A softer 5-year staleness signal is computed internally (stale_status_count) but not yet surfaced in the UI: only the 2-year "Frozen" state is currently shown to users.
  • Recurring signatures can conflate a real repeating problem with a generic repair code. One order code ("repair with similar material...") covered 20 different structural defects at a single building. The narrative wording flags this when a signature's descriptions don't cohere, but the Persistent/Chronic bucket counts don't require that coherence check yet, only the sentence does.
  • Administrative-filing exclusion isn't exhaustive. Only the top 60 of 396 OrderNumber codes have been manually audited for hidden compliance-calendar patterns like the bedbug-report case; the current exclusion list covers 7 codes.
  • There's no guard yet against genuinely implausible "still open" deadlines (e.g. a corrupted pre-1990 date) skewing a building's backlog-age calculation. Date sanity-checking exists elsewhere in the pipeline (see Data) but not specifically on this field.
  • Class "I" notices (Orders to Repair/Vacate) carry no correction deadline and count toward Scale but not toward Backlog age.
  • Severity's "Elevated floor" rule (any currently-open, uncertified Class C blocks a "Low" read) requires per-violation certification status that isn't in the map's citywide summary file, so the Pattern × Severity crosstab above uses a simplified rate-only version, not the exact live rule.
