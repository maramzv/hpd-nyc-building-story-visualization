About

Open violations hold the evidence.
Patterns make it readable.

NYC Building Stories is an interactive data investigation of New York City's currently open HPD housing violations. It turns millions of public records into citywide patterns and building-level stories, while keeping the underlying evidence close enough to inspect.

What can open housing-violation records tell us about the way problems appear, recur, and persist across NYC buildings?

The project does not try to reduce a building to a score. It asks what kind of record the building has, how that record has changed over time, and how it compares with patterns visible across the city.

Why build this?

NYC Open Data makes the underlying records public, but a row-by-row feed is difficult to read as a building story. One row can tell you what one violation is, when it was issued, and its current status. What it doesn't tell you on its own is whether the same problem keeps returning, whether a building's open record is mostly recent or dominated by older unresolved violations, whether severe violations make up a meaningful share, or whether the building's pattern is common or unusual in its neighborhood.

Even a raw violation count doesn't solve that problem. A building with 40 open violations might have dozens of unrelated one-time issues, while another building with the same count might show the same defect returning again and again over many years. The number is identical; the underlying story isn't.

And at city scale, the problem gets harder. Millions of individual records make it possible to look up what happened, but not easy to see the larger structure inside the data: which problems recur, where long-running patterns concentrate, how recent activity differs from old backlog, or when high volume reflects many separate issues rather than one problem repeating.

That's the gap this project is designed to close. It groups records by building, separates different dimensions of the record, and looks for patterns that raw counts flatten together.

Isolated · Extreme severity

A building may have a single currently open Class C violation, HPD's immediately hazardous category, with no recurring defect visible in the open record.

Chronic · Low severity

Another building may have the same non-hazardous defect cited ten or more times across at least five years. The individual violations may be lower severity, but the recurrence tells a very different story.

A single number can't describe both cases well. That's why this project keeps recurrence, severity, recency, scale, engagement, and backlog age separate instead of collapsing them into one rating.

What can I investigate?

The project moves from citywide patterns to the individual records behind a building's story. You can start by asking what's happening across New York City, then narrow the view from borough to neighborhood to a single building and inspect the evidence behind what you see.

Across the city
  • Where are recurring building problems most concentrated?
  • How do violation patterns differ across boroughs and neighborhoods?
  • Where does high violation volume reflect recurrence, and where does it not?
  • How does one building compare with the area around it?
At a building
  • Scale: is the open record small or extensive?
  • Recency: are violations mostly recent, or does the record stretch back years?
  • Severity: how much of the record is Class C, HPD's highest hazard class?
  • Engagement: has the owner been responding to violations?
  • Pattern: does the same kind of defect keep reappearing?
  • Backlog age: how far back do the oldest open violations go?
  • Evidence: what individual records produced the building's story?
  • Timeline: how has the record unfolded over time?

How do I read the visualization?

Two visual encodings are working at the same time. Color describes the building's pattern. Height can be switched between different lenses, depending on the question you want to ask.

Switching lenses changes height, not color. A building keeps its Pattern classification across all five lenses.

"Chronic" is a pattern in the open violation record, not a building grade. It means the same specific defect has been cited ten or more times across at least five years. It doesn't mean every condition at the building is dangerous, and it isn't a recommendation about whether someone should live there.

Where does the evidence come from?

Building stories aren't meant to be black boxes. The core source is NYC Open Data's Open HPD Violations dataset (csn4-vhvf). Records are grouped by building and then used to calculate the dimensions and pattern classification shown in the interface.

The building view lets you move from the generated summary to the underlying violation records, including the dates, classes, descriptions, and status history that support it. The Methodology page documents how the classifications and thresholds are built, and the Data page documents the source, fields, cleaning rules, and known limitations.

What shouldn't I conclude from this?

This project does
  • Analyze patterns in currently open HPD violations
  • Separate recurrence, severity, recency, scale, engagement, and backlog age
  • Compare building patterns across neighborhoods and boroughs
  • Keep building-level conclusions connected to the records behind them
This project doesn't
  • Measure the complete safety or condition of a building
  • Rank landlords or recommend where someone should live
  • Explain why a neighborhood or borough has the pattern it does
The most important limitation: the source feed contains currently open violations, not a complete archive of every violation ever issued and resolved. "No recurring defect found" means no recurring defect is visible in this open-violation feed. It isn't the same thing as a clean historical record or a clean bill of health.
