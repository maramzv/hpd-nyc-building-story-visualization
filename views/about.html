About

Open violations hold the evidence.
Patterns reveal the shape of a city.

NYC's Open HPD Violations dataset holds millions of housing-violation records. On their own, that's just a pile of rows. Understanding what they actually say about any one building is the harder problem, and the one this project exists to answer.

What is happening to NYC's buildings?

NYC Building Stories reads millions of housing-violation records for what recurs, what escalates, and what quietly persists, then makes every finding traceable back to the record it came from, from a single address to the city as a whole.

Not "this building has 42 violations." A raw count says almost nothing on its own. We're after the transformations that turn a pile of records into an actual pattern: recurrence, persistence, severity, concentration, and change over time. Those are the shapes that make one building's story different from another's, even when their violation counts look the same.

The site is built around that idea, top to bottom: start at the citywide pattern, narrow to a borough, then a neighborhood, then a single building. At every zoom level, the claim being made is only as strong as the evidence behind it. Every building's story links back to the actual violation records it was generated from, so the underlying evidence is never more than a click away.

Why patterns beat raw counts

Two buildings can carry the same number on paper and mean something completely different underneath it. That's the reason this project measures recurrence and severity separately instead of folding them into one score:

Isolated · Extreme severity

A single Class C violation, an immediately hazardous condition like no heat or a gas leak, with nothing else on record. It's dangerous right now, but there's no history of it, or anything else, recurring.

Chronic · Low severity

The same non-hazardous defect cited ten or more times over five years. Nothing about it is immediately dangerous, but it's gone unresolved for the better part of a decade.

Both are real, and neither one is "worse" in a way a single number could capture. Treating them the same would erase the exact distinction the records are making.

Four zoom levels, one question

The map is built as a drill-down, and each level answers a different scope of the same question:

Citywide → Borough → Neighborhood → Building

Click a borough and the map flies there while its row expands to show its top neighborhoods. Click a neighborhood to fly in closer. Click any building's 3D column for its full evidence-based story, or search an address directly if you already know where you're headed. A "Why we're saying this" link on every building opens the actual violation records the story was built from.

Five ways to look at the same buildings

The height of every column on the map is a choice, not a fixed property of the data. You can switch what it represents:

Buildings
Real height, from NYC footprint data.
Violations
Count of currently open violations.
Age
Years past deadline for the oldest open item.
Severity
Count of Class C (immediately hazardous) violations.
Recent
Violations issued in the last 2 years.

The color underneath never changes: it's always the Pattern bucket (see Methodology). Only what the height means changes.

A model shaped by testing

The pattern taxonomy wasn't fixed in advance. It changed as testing against the full citywide dataset exposed cases the earlier version couldn't represent:

  1. 01
    Start with recurrence. An early three-bucket model, Isolated, Persistent, and Chronic, grouped violations by building and looked for the same defect code recurring over time.
  2. 02
    Expand to the full population. Testing across all 166,000+ buildings surfaced two missing cases, buildings with only administrative filings and buildings with many one-off defects but no repeat, which became No real defects and Widespread.
  3. 03
    Move recurrence to the building level. The same defect showing up across different apartments in one building is still one recurring problem, not several unrelated ones.
  4. 04
    Stop compressing into one label. Scale, recency, severity, engagement, pattern, and backlog age move independently, so the final model reports all six rather than collapsing them into a single score.

What the patterns reveal

Once recurrence is separated from raw volume, it stops being evenly spread across the map.

2.7×
Persistent and Chronic patterns show up in roughly 26% of mapped buildings with an open violation in the Bronx and Manhattan, versus about 10% in Queens and Staten Island. In a few neighborhoods in northern Manhattan and the west Bronx, it's over half. That's a pattern in the records, not an explanation for it: ownership, enforcement, and reporting behavior are outside this dataset's scope. Full breakdown in Methodology.

Who this is for

Anyone trying to understand NYC housing conditions through public data, with an evidence-backed exploration of what the data shows, not a curated argument for a conclusion decided in advance.

This is
  • An honest exploration of a public dataset
  • Patterns tested against real buildings before being shown
  • Full transparency into the records behind every claim
This isn't
  • A "should I live here" score or recommendation engine
  • A ranking of landlords or a legal record
  • A complete picture of building safety or condition

One more limit worth stating plainly: the source feed holds currently open violations, not a full history of every violation ever issued. A building with no recurring defect found here has no open recurring defect, which isn't the same as a clean record. More on that in Data.
