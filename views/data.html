Scale

This isn't a sample. The map loads the full citywide population: every one of the 166,481 buildings (current production dataset) with at least one currently-open HPD violation, drawn from a source pull of roughly 2.9 million violation records across 129,405 property registrations. Because the underlying feed updates continuously, the exact building and record counts drift slightly with every re-pull. The number below is read live off the file the map itself loads, not a fixed historical figure.

A property registration is HPD's ownership filing, not a location record, and one registration can cover several buildings under one owner. That's why this count is lower than the building count above.

The single most important caveat about this dataset: "Open HPD Violations" (csn4-vhvf) contains only currently-unresolved violations, not a full history of every violation ever issued. A cross-check against a related NYC Open Data dataset confirmed the API is server-side filtered to exclude closed or dismissed violations entirely. So a building with "no recurring defect found" here has no open recurring defect on this feed, which is not a clean bill of health, and not a record of everything that was ever cited and fixed.

What's in a violation record

Each row is one HPD violation, not one building and not one inspection visit.

2.92M
Rows in the raw feed
30
Columns in the raw feed

This project reads 21 of those 30 columns and drops 9. Every field falls into one of four categories, each with a specific job in building the map:

  • Identity & grouping: turns thousands of loose records into one building's story.
  • Location: puts that building's dot on the map, in the right neighborhood.
  • Defect & severity: decides the color you see on that building.
  • Timeline & status: fills the evidence view when you click it.

That's the order below: group the records, place the building, color it, then show its history. Each category gets a table listing every field in it, what it is, whether it's used, and why, plus a line of text only where a field needs more than the table can say.

Grouped, placed, colored, timed. That's the full chain from a raw HPD row to a building's story on the map. Where each of those pieces actually comes from is below, if you want to check it yourself.

What was added to make it mappable

HPD's violation records don't come with coordinates, a building shape, or a neighborhood name. Four of these sources supply content you actually see on the map; the last one exists only to bridge one dataset's ID system to another's.

SourceIDUsed forVerify it
NYC GeoSearch None (API service) Precise per-address coordinates, preferred over PLUTO. 166,372/166,481 (99.93%) geocoded geosearch.planninglabs.nyc →
NYC PLUTO 64uk-42ks Floor count, footprint area (fallback shape when Building Footprints geometry is missing), and fallback coordinates for the small remainder GeoSearch couldn't match View on NYC Open Data →
NYC NTA Boundaries 9nt8-h7nd Neighborhood assignment, point-in-polygon View on NYC Open Data →
NYC Building Footprints 5zhs-2jue Real building shape + roof height. 99.4% matched, powers the "Buildings" height lens View on NYC Open Data →
HPD Buildings Subject to HPD Jurisdiction kj4p-ruqc ID bridge only: resolves buildingid to BIN/BBL; no content of its own shown on the map View on NYC Open Data →

All 4 dataset publishers here are NYC city agencies (Dept. of City Planning; Office of Technology and Innovation for Footprints; HPD for the jurisdiction bridge). Each link above goes straight to that agency's own listing on NYC Open Data.

A known rendering rule: a small number of buildings still end up sharing an exact coordinate, either a PLUTO lot-centroid fallback shared by several buildings, or, more rarely, two addresses GeoSearch geocoded to the same point. Currently 1,417 buildings (0.85%), in 547 groups, are affected (largest group: 19 buildings sharing one point). Each group is spread into a small grid, scaled to the group's own building size, so every building stays visible and individually clickable. This is a display adjustment, not a change to any building's underlying data, and the count shrinks further each time more buildings get a real geocoded address.
