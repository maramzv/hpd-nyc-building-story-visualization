Scale

This isn't a sample. The map loads the full citywide population: every one of the 166,481 buildings (current production dataset) with at least one currently-open HPD violation, drawn from a source pull of roughly 2.9 million violation records across 129,405 property registrations. Because the underlying feed updates continuously, the exact building and record counts drift slightly with every re-pull. The number below is read live off the file the map itself loads, not a fixed historical figure.

The single most important caveat about this dataset: "Open HPD Violations" (csn4-vhvf) contains only currently-unresolved violations, not a full history of every violation ever issued. A cross-check against a related NYC Open Data dataset confirmed the API is server-side filtered to exclude closed or dismissed violations entirely. So a building with "no recurring defect found" here has no open recurring defect on this feed, which is not a clean bill of health, and not a record of everything that was ever cited and fixed.

What's actually in a violation record

Each row is one HPD violation, not one building and not one inspection visit. A few fields drive everything downstream:

BuildingID is confirmed reliable for grouping records into buildings, the core assumption the whole project depends on. OrderNumber isn't a unique ID despite the name; it's one of only 396 standardized problem-category codes shared across unrelated buildings citywide, which is what makes it possible to detect the same kind of defect recurring at a building over time (see Methodology).

HPD's own administrative turnaround is fast: inspection to notice issuance is a median of 2–3 days. Fix-by deadlines, measured directly from the data rather than from an external secondary source, run shorter for the most urgent class than commonly assumed: Class A a median 95 days, Class B 35 days, Class C 14 days. Heat and hot water violations move on an even faster internal track within Class C.

Certification is uncommon, but not because owners who attempt it respond late; most who do certify are close to on time. It's uncommon because most violations are never certified at all. And for that larger uncertified population, a plurality have had no recorded activity of any kind: no certification, no reinspection, no status update, in over five years. This is the reason the evidence timeline's "Frozen" state exists (using a shorter 2-year silence threshold, so it flags sooner than the 5-year figure above): the dataset's own concept of "open" means "never formally closed," not "actively being worked on right now."

Violations are heavily concentrated, not evenly spread

The average of ~17.4 violations per building is misleading on its own. The current top building, 530 East 169th Street, Bronx, has 1,268 open violations by itself. Meanwhile the citywide median is just 6, and 35.8% of buildings have 3 or fewer open violations. A handful of buildings carry a wildly disproportionate share of the total, which is exactly why this project doesn't rank buildings by raw count.

The identity of "the most-cited building" is not a fixed fact about the city; it's a snapshot of whatever this feed currently shows.

Data cleaning rules applied before analysis

A few properties of the raw feed require explicit handling before violation counts mean what they appear to mean:

  • Same-day duplicate filings. A single underlying defect is sometimes filed as more than one record on the same day. Occurrences are deduplicated by building + apartment + description + issue date before anything is counted as a separate recurrence.
  • Certification data is sparse by design, not incomplete. 88.7% of records have no CertifiedDate, because most open violations simply haven't had a certification attempt yet. This reflects where each violation sits in HPD's process, not missing data.
  • Date fields are range-validated. The raw feed contains a small number of dates outside any plausible calendar range. All date-based analysis filters to a sane window before any min/max or trend calculation runs on it.
  • Regulatory filings are distinguished from physical defects. A small set of order codes recur by design on a fixed compliance calendar (an annual bedbug-report filing, a registration renewal) rather than because a physical problem keeps happening. These are identified by OrderNumber and excluded from recurrence detection (details in Methodology).

What was added

HPD's violation records don't come with coordinates or building shape, so two more public datasets fill that in:

Location + floor heightsNYC PLUTO dataset (~99.75% match rate at build time)
Neighborhood boundariesNYC NTA boundaries, point-in-polygon; every one of the 166,481 buildings currently mapped carries a neighborhood
Building footprint sizeEstimated from PLUTO's building area & floor count, bucketed into 5 size tiers

A small share of buildings couldn't be matched to coordinates (mostly condo/complex sub-lots) and are excluded from the map entirely, rather than shown in the wrong place. The PLUTO match-rate figure above is from the pipeline's build step, not recomputed on every data refresh the way the pattern and severity numbers on this page are.

A known rendering rule: PLUTO assigns one coordinate per tax lot, so distinct HPD buildings sharing a lot (9,805 buildings, 5.9% of the dataset) would otherwise render as a single stacked column. These are spread into a small ring around the shared point so each building stays visible and individually clickable. This is a display adjustment, not a change to any building's underlying data.
